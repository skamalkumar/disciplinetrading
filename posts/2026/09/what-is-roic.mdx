## What Is ROIC?

ROIC stands for Return on Invested Capital. It is a profitability and efficiency metric used to assess how effectively a company generates operating returns from the capital invested in its business.

In simple terms:

**ROIC asks how efficiently a company turns invested capital into operating profit after considering taxes.**

This makes ROIC useful when studying the underlying economics of a business rather than looking only at its share price.

## How Is ROIC Calculated?

A commonly used framework is:

**ROIC = NOPAT ÷ Invested Capital × 100**

Where:

- **NOPAT** means Net Operating Profit After Tax.
- **Invested Capital** represents the capital invested in the operating business.

Different analysts may use slightly different definitions for NOPAT and invested capital. The important point is to use a consistent methodology when comparing companies.

## Simple ROIC Example

Suppose a company generates:

- NOPAT: ₹150 crore
- Invested Capital: ₹750 crore

Then:

**ROIC = ₹150 crore ÷ ₹750 crore × 100 = 20%**

The company is generating a 20% return on its invested operating capital under those assumptions.

## Why ROIC Matters

Revenue and profit tell investors how much a company earns.

ROIC adds another question:

**How efficiently does the company generate those earnings from the capital invested in the business?**

Two companies can generate similar profits but require very different amounts of capital to do so.

That difference can be important when evaluating business quality.

## ROIC and Competitive Advantage

A company that can consistently earn strong returns on invested capital may have an attractive business model.

Potential sources of strong returns include:

- Pricing power
- Efficient asset utilisation
- Strong customer relationships
- Brand strength
- Low capital requirements
- Competitive barriers

However, a high ROIC does not automatically mean a stock is attractively valued.

Investors should also examine valuation, growth, financial strength and cash generation.

## ROIC vs ROE

ROE measures the return generated on shareholders' equity.

ROIC focuses more broadly on the operating capital invested in the business.

This distinction matters because financial leverage can influence ROE.

For example, a company can increase ROE through greater use of debt, while ROIC can provide a different view of the economics of the underlying business.

See [What Is ROE?](/blog/what-is-roe) for more on shareholder returns.

## ROIC vs ROCE

ROIC and ROCE are closely related concepts, but they are not identical.

ROCE is commonly based on EBIT divided by capital employed.

ROIC commonly uses NOPAT divided by invested capital.

Because definitions can vary between analysts and financial databases, investors should check the methodology before comparing reported numbers.

For a broader explanation of capital efficiency, read [What Is ROCE?](/blog/what-is-roce).

## ROIC and Growth

Growth is not automatically valuable.

If a company grows by investing capital and earns attractive returns on that additional capital, growth can potentially create substantial value.

But if growth requires large amounts of capital while returns on that capital remain weak, growth may not translate into attractive economics for shareholders.

This is why ROIC can be useful alongside [revenue](/blog/what-is-revenue), [profit margin](/blog/what-is-profit-margin), and [free cash flow](/blog/what-is-free-cash-flow).

## What Is a Good ROIC?

There is no single ROIC level that is appropriate for every business.

Investors should consider:

- Industry economics
- Capital intensity
- Business maturity
- Competitive position
- Historical ROIC
- Cost of capital

A particularly useful approach is to study whether ROIC remains consistently above the company's cost of capital over a long period.

## Common Mistakes

### Looking at One Year

One year's ROIC may be affected by unusual profits, asset sales or temporary conditions.

### Ignoring Capital Requirements

A business may have attractive margins but require substantial reinvestment.

### Confusing ROIC With Valuation

A high-quality company can still be an expensive stock.

### Comparing Inconsistent Calculations

Different data providers may define invested capital differently.

## How Beginners Can Use ROIC

Use ROIC as part of a broader framework:

1. Examine the company's historical ROIC.
2. Compare it with competitors.
3. Study whether ROIC is stable or improving.
4. Review margins and operating profitability.
5. Examine free cash flow and reinvestment requirements.
6. Consider debt and the balance sheet.
7. Finally, assess the stock's valuation.

## Final Thoughts

ROIC is one of the useful metrics for understanding the economics of a business.

Rather than asking only whether a company is growing, investors can ask whether that growth is producing attractive returns on the capital required to achieve it.
