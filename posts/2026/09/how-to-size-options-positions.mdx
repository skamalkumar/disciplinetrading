## What Is Position Sizing?

Position sizing is the process of deciding how many units or lots to trade. In options, it should be based on the loss that could occur under the strategy's risk definition, not simply on the premium collected or the margin displayed by a broker.

A sound position size helps keep one trade from causing disproportionate damage to the trading account.

## Start With a Risk Budget

Suppose a hypothetical trading account has ₹5,00,000 and the trader sets a maximum planned risk of 0.5% of account equity for one trade.

\[	ext{Risk Budget} = ₹5,00,000 	imes 0.5\% = ₹2,500\]

This is an illustrative risk policy, not a recommended percentage for every trader.

## Position Sizing for a Defined-Risk Spread

Assume a credit spread has a maximum loss of 40 points per unit after net credit, and the applicable contract multiplier is 50. The maximum loss per lot is:

\[40 	imes 50 = ₹2,000\]

With a ₹2,500 risk budget, the mathematical limit is one lot because two lots would create a maximum loss of ₹4,000. The trader should also allow for costs, execution slippage and any risk not captured by the simplified payoff calculation.

## Long Options Position Sizing

For a fully paid long Call or Put, the premium paid is generally the maximum contractual loss at expiry, excluding costs and subject to the product's terms. If a Call costs 80 points and the contract multiplier is 50, one lot costs ₹4,000 before charges. If the planned risk budget is lower than that amount, the trader should not increase the risk budget simply to fit the trade.

## Why Margin Is Not Maximum Risk

Broker margin is collateral required to carry a position. It is not necessarily the maximum amount that can be lost. Unhedged short options and futures can produce losses much larger than the initial margin. Margin requirements can also rise during volatile markets.

## Position Sizing for Undefined-Risk Positions

Naked short options can have very large or theoretically unlimited risk depending on the contract. A simple fixed-risk calculation may not be adequate. Traders should understand stress scenarios, margin changes, gap risk, liquidity and the possibility that stop orders may execute at worse prices than expected. Defined-risk structures can make the payoff boundary clearer, but they still carry execution and operational risks.

## A Practical Sizing Process

1. Define the strategy and all legs before entry.
2. Calculate the maximum loss or a conservative stress loss.
3. Multiply point risk by the current contract multiplier and number of lots.
4. Add estimated brokerage, exchange charges, taxes and slippage.
5. Compare the result with a pre-set account risk budget.
6. Check liquidity and whether the position can be exited in stressed conditions.
7. Reassess size if account equity or market conditions change.

## Avoid These Position-Sizing Errors

- Choosing lot size based on the premium received.
- Treating margin as the maximum possible loss.
- Ignoring the combined risk of correlated positions.
- Increasing size after losses to recover quickly.
- Using an outdated lot size or contract specification.
- Assuming a stop-loss guarantees the intended exit price.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I risk the same amount on every options trade?

A consistent framework can improve discipline, but risk should also reflect strategy structure, liquidity and market conditions.

### Can I use the premium received to calculate risk for a short option?

No. Premium received is not the maximum risk for an uncovered short option.

### Does a defined-risk spread eliminate all risk?

No. It defines the theoretical expiry payoff under the contract terms, but execution, costs, settlement and operational risks remain.

### Should position size change after a drawdown?

A risk policy may require reducing size as account equity falls. The important point is to define the rule in advance rather than react emotionally.

## Final Thoughts

Position sizing connects a trading idea to the amount of capital at risk. Calculate risk from the complete strategy, verify current contract details and keep the potential loss within a pre-defined limit.
