## What Is a Poor Man's Covered Call?

A **Poor Man's Covered Call (PMCC)** is an options strategy designed to resemble a covered call without purchasing 100 shares (or the applicable share quantity) of the underlying. It typically involves buying a longer-dated, deep-in-the-money Call and selling a nearer-dated Call at a higher strike.

The long Call acts as a stock substitute, but it is not identical to owning shares. It has an expiry, carries time value and has its own Delta, Gamma and Vega exposure. The strategy is a form of Call diagonal spread.

## How Is a PMCC Built?

A common structure is:

1. Buy a longer-dated Call, often with a relatively high Delta.
2. Sell a nearer-dated Call at a higher strike.
3. Manage or close the short Call before or around its expiry according to a predefined plan.

The long option is sometimes called the *LEAPS* leg in markets where long-dated listed options are available. The available expiries and contract specifications differ across exchanges.

## Illustrative Example

Assume a stock trades at ₹1,000. A trader buys a longer-dated 800 Call for ₹240 and sells a nearer-dated 1,050 Call for ₹35.

The initial net debit is ₹205 per share, excluding costs. The short Call premium reduces the cost of the long option, but it does not make the trade risk-free. If the stock falls sharply, the long Call can lose substantial value. If the stock rallies strongly above the short strike, the short Call can limit near-term upside and may require management.

## Why Traders Consider a PMCC

- It can require less upfront capital than buying the underlying shares.
- The short Call may generate premium that offsets some of the long Call's time decay.
- The position can express a moderately bullish view.
- It can be adjusted by closing, rolling or replacing the short Call, subject to costs and risk.

These are potential characteristics, not guaranteed outcomes.

## Understanding Breakeven and Risk

The simple entry debit is the long Call premium minus the short Call premium. But the actual breakeven at a particular date depends on the value of the long Call, the short Call's closing or settlement value, remaining time and transaction costs.

Do not calculate PMCC risk as though the long Call were ordinary shares. A long Call can lose its entire premium. Also, a short Call can create a significant obligation if the underlying rises sharply. The long Call may not perfectly offset the short Call because strikes, expiries and Greeks differ.

## Choosing the Long Call

Traders often look for a longer-dated Call with substantial intrinsic value and a relatively high Delta. A higher Delta may make it behave more like shares for small price changes, but Delta is not fixed. The option can still be affected by time decay, volatility and a large underlying move.

## Choosing the Short Call

The short strike and expiry should reflect the trader's outlook and tolerance for upside being capped. A short option with very little time remaining may decay quickly, but can also have high Gamma near expiry. A short Call should not be selected only because its premium looks attractive.

## PMCC vs Traditional Covered Call

| Feature | PMCC | Covered call |
|---|---|---|
| Long exposure | Long-dated Call | Underlying shares |
| Short leg | Nearer-dated Call | Call option |
| Expiry on long exposure | Yes | Shares do not expire |
| Main extra risk | Long option time/volatility risk | Share-price risk |

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a PMCC safer than a covered call?

Not automatically. It uses an option instead of shares, introducing expiry and volatility risks. The total risk depends on the structure and management.

### Can a PMCC lose the full debit?

Yes. The long Call can expire worthless, and losses on the overall position can be substantial relative to the debit depending on how the short leg is managed and settled.

### Is PMCC suitable for every stock?

No. Liquidity, option availability, volatility, spreads and contract size all matter.
