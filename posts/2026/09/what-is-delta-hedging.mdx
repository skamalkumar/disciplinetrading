## What Is Delta Hedging?

Delta hedging is a method of adjusting a position so that its overall sensitivity to small movements in the underlying asset is reduced. Traders estimate this sensitivity using **Delta**, one of the option Greeks.

A call option generally has positive Delta and a put option generally has negative Delta. A portfolio containing options can therefore have a net Delta that indicates its approximate directional exposure at that moment.

For a foundation, read [What Is Option Delta?](https://disciplinetrading.com/blog/what-is-option-delta) and [What Are Option Greeks?](https://disciplinetrading.com/blog/what-are-option-greeks).

## A Simple Example

Suppose a trader holds options with a combined net Delta of +200 in the relevant contract-adjusted units. The portfolio would generally gain value from a small rise in the underlying and lose value from a small fall, all else equal.

The trader might use an appropriate quantity of the underlying instrument or a related futures contract to offset some of that positive exposure. The precise hedge quantity depends on the contract multiplier, instrument, portfolio and broker/exchange specifications; the number 200 alone is not enough to determine an order quantity.

After hedging, the net Delta may be closer to zero. This is called being **delta neutral** at that point in time.

## Why Delta Hedging Is Not Permanent Protection

Delta changes as the underlying price moves, time passes and implied volatility changes. Gamma measures how quickly Delta changes when the underlying moves. A hedge that is effective now may become imperfect after the market moves.

This means delta hedging is usually a process of monitoring and adjustment rather than a one-time action.

## Who Uses Delta Hedging?

- Market makers managing option inventory
- Institutions managing portfolios of derivatives
- Traders seeking to reduce directional exposure
- Advanced options traders managing multi-leg positions

A retail trader can learn the concept without assuming that frequent hedging is practical or cost-effective for every account.

## Delta Hedging vs Directional Trading

A directional trader may deliberately maintain positive or negative Delta to express a market view. A delta hedger tries to reduce that exposure, at least temporarily. A hedge can therefore change the purpose of a position from directional speculation toward risk management.

## Costs and Limitations

Delta hedging does not remove Gamma, Vega, Theta, liquidity, basis or execution risk. Frequent adjustments can also incur brokerage, taxes, bid-ask spread and slippage. A hedge using a related instrument may not track the original exposure perfectly.

Before hedging, identify what risk you are trying to reduce, what instrument can offset it, how often it may need adjustment and what the total costs could be.

## Practical Checklist

1. Calculate the position's net Delta using the correct contract multipliers.
2. Decide what exposure you want to offset and what residual risk is acceptable.
3. Select a hedge instrument whose behaviour and contract terms you understand.
4. Monitor changes in Delta, Gamma, volatility and liquidity.
5. Include transaction costs and stress scenarios in the plan.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does delta hedging guarantee a risk-free position?

No. It can reduce directional sensitivity to small price changes at a particular time, but other risks remain and Delta changes.

### Is delta-neutral the same as risk-neutral?

No. Delta neutrality describes one sensitivity measure. A position can still have substantial Gamma, Vega, Theta, liquidity and gap risk.

### Can futures be used to delta hedge options?

Potentially, where the futures contract is an appropriate hedge for the option exposure. Contract multipliers, basis risk and expiry differences must be considered.
