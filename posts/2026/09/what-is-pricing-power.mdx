# What Is Pricing Power?

**Pricing power** is a company's ability to raise prices without losing so many customers or sales that the increase damages its economics. It can arise from brand preference, differentiated products, switching costs, limited competition, or an essential role in a customer's process.

## Why pricing power matters

A company with pricing power may be better able to protect margins when wages, materials, logistics, or other input costs rise. But the result depends on how customers respond and whether competitors can undercut the new price.

## How investors can look for evidence

- Compare gross and operating margins across several years and different cost environments.
- Review volume growth alongside price or revenue growth where the company discloses it.
- Check whether price increases are followed by customer losses or weaker demand.
- Compare performance with relevant competitors.
- Read management commentary critically and test it against reported results.

Revenue growth alone does not prove pricing power. Growth may come from acquisitions, currency movements, new capacity, or higher volumes. Likewise, rising margins can reflect temporary input-cost relief rather than durable pricing strength.

## A simple illustration

Suppose a company's unit costs rise by 5%. It increases selling prices by 6%, while sales volumes remain broadly stable. This may indicate some pricing power, but investors should check product mix, competitor actions, and whether the pattern persists. The illustration is not a forecast.

## Risks and limitations

Aggressive price increases can reduce demand, invite competition, attract regulatory attention, or damage customer trust. Pricing power can vary by product, geography, and economic cycle.

## Key takeaway

Pricing power is best assessed through evidence on prices, volumes, margins, customer behaviour, and competitors—not through management claims alone.

## FAQs

### Is pricing power the same as having a premium brand?
No. A brand can help, but pricing power may also come from switching costs, product differentiation, scarcity, or the cost of changing suppliers.

### Can a company have pricing power during a recession?
Possibly, but customer sensitivity and product demand can change. Evidence should be reviewed across different conditions.

### Does pricing power always increase profits?
Not necessarily. Higher prices may be offset by lower volumes, higher costs, or competitive responses.
