## What Is the Current Ratio?

The Current Ratio is a liquidity ratio that measures a company's ability to meet its short-term obligations using its current assets.

In simple terms, it asks:

**Does the company have enough short-term assets to cover its short-term liabilities?**

Because companies have bills, suppliers, salaries, taxes and other obligations that may become due within a relatively short period, liquidity is an important part of fundamental analysis.

## How Is the Current Ratio Calculated?

The formula is:

**Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities**

Current assets can include:

- Cash and cash equivalents
- Trade receivables
- Inventory
- Short-term investments
- Other assets expected to be converted into cash within the operating cycle

Current liabilities can include:

- Trade payables
- Short-term borrowings
- Accrued expenses
- Other obligations due in the short term

## Simple Example

Suppose a company has:

- Current Assets: ₹500 crore
- Current Liabilities: ₹250 crore

Then:

**Current Ratio = ₹500 crore ÷ ₹250 crore = 2.0**

A ratio of 2 means the company has ₹2 of current assets for every ₹1 of current liabilities.

## What Does a High Current Ratio Mean?

A relatively high Current Ratio can indicate a stronger short-term liquidity position.

However, higher is not automatically better.

A very high ratio may sometimes indicate that the company is holding excess cash, inventory or receivables that are not being used efficiently.

Therefore, investors should examine what makes up the current assets.

## What Does a Low Current Ratio Mean?

A lower Current Ratio can indicate that the company has less short-term asset coverage against its current obligations.

That does not automatically mean the company is financially weak.

Some businesses operate successfully with lower current ratios because they have:

- Fast inventory turnover
- Strong cash generation
- Predictable customer payments
- Strong bargaining power with suppliers
- Recurring revenue

The industry and business model matter.

## Is a Current Ratio Above 1 Good?

A Current Ratio above 1 generally means current assets exceed current liabilities.

But investors should not use 1.0 as a universal pass-or-fail level.

A company's historical ratio and industry peers can provide much more useful context.

For example, a business with a stable Current Ratio around 1.2 may be normal for its industry, while another company with a sudden fall from 2.0 to 1.1 could deserve closer investigation.

## Current Ratio vs Quick Ratio

The Current Ratio includes inventory in current assets.

The Quick Ratio is more conservative because it generally excludes inventory and focuses on assets that are more readily convertible into cash.

This distinction matters because inventory may take time to sell.

Investors should therefore consider both measures when liquidity risk is important.

## Current Ratio vs Debt-to-Equity Ratio

These ratios measure different aspects of financial strength.

**Current Ratio:** focuses on short-term liquidity.

**Debt-to-Equity:** focuses on financial leverage and the relationship between debt and shareholders' equity.

You can read more about leverage in [What Is Debt-to-Equity Ratio?](/blog/what-is-debt-to-equity-ratio).

## Why Investors Should Track the Trend

One Current Ratio number gives only a snapshot.

A multi-year trend can be more informative.

If the ratio is steadily declining, investors can investigate whether:

- Current liabilities are increasing
- Cash balances are falling
- Receivables are rising
- Inventory is accumulating
- Short-term borrowing is increasing

The ratio should therefore be interpreted together with the company's balance sheet and cash flows.

## Current Ratio and Free Cash Flow

Liquidity analysis should not stop with accounting ratios.

A company that consistently generates strong cash flows may have more financial flexibility than a company whose current assets are heavily concentrated in slow-moving inventory or receivables.

See [What Is Free Cash Flow?](/blog/what-is-free-cash-flow) for a deeper explanation of cash generation.

## Common Mistakes

### Treating 1.0 as a Universal Rule

The appropriate level differs across industries.

### Ignoring Asset Quality

₹100 crore of cash is not economically identical to ₹100 crore of slow-moving inventory.

### Looking Only at One Year

Trends often provide more useful information.

### Ignoring Cash Flow

Liquidity ratios should be supported by actual cash-generation analysis.

## How Beginners Can Use Current Ratio

A simple approach is:

1. Calculate the Current Ratio.
2. Compare it with previous years.
3. Compare it with similar companies.
4. Examine the composition of current assets.
5. Review short-term debt and other current liabilities.
6. Check operating cash flow and free cash flow.
7. Investigate significant changes rather than relying on one number.

## Final Thoughts

The Current Ratio is a simple but useful starting point for understanding short-term financial strength.

It should not be used alone. A disciplined investor combines liquidity analysis with profitability, leverage, cash flow and valuation measures.
