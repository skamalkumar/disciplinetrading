## What Is Sum-of-the-Parts Valuation?

Sum-of-the-Parts (SOTP) valuation is a method used to estimate the value of a company by valuing its major businesses or assets separately and then combining those values. It is particularly useful when a company operates in several businesses with different growth rates, margins, risks or valuation characteristics.

A conglomerate with a consumer business, a financial-services arm and an industrial division may not be well represented by applying one valuation multiple to the entire group. SOTP attempts to reflect the economics of each segment.

## How the Method Works

1. Identify the material operating segments and non-operating assets.
2. Select an appropriate valuation method for each segment, such as comparable-company multiples, discounted cash flow or asset-based valuation.
3. Estimate the value of each segment using consistent financial data and clearly stated assumptions.
4. Add the segment values and separately identified investments or surplus assets.
5. Deduct net debt, minority interests and other claims not already reflected.
6. Divide the resulting equity value by diluted shares outstanding to estimate value per share.

**Illustrative formula:** Equity value = sum of segment values + non-operating assets − net debt − other claims.

## Worked Example

Assume a company has three divisions valued at ₹4,000 crore, ₹2,500 crore and ₹1,500 crore. It also holds surplus investments worth ₹500 crore. Net debt is ₹1,200 crore and minority interests are ₹300 crore.

The illustrative equity value is ₹7,000 crore (₹4,000 + ₹2,500 + ₹1,500 + ₹500 − ₹1,200 − ₹300). If diluted shares are 100 crore, the implied value is ₹70 per share. These figures are hypothetical and are not a valuation of an actual company.

## Choosing Segment Valuation Methods

- Stable, cash-generative businesses may be assessed using earnings or cash-flow multiples.
- High-growth businesses may require scenario-based DCF or carefully selected peer multiples.
- Financial businesses often need sector-specific measures rather than enterprise-value-to-EBITDA.
- Property, investments and other non-operating assets may require asset-specific valuation.

## Common Mistakes

- Applying an unjustifiably high multiple to a fast-growing segment.
- Using inconsistent dates, currencies or accounting definitions.
- Counting the same cash flow or asset twice.
- Forgetting corporate costs, tax leakage, debt, leases or minority interests.
- Treating a theoretical SOTP discount as proof that the stock must rerate.

## SOTP Discount and Conglomerate Discount

A market price below estimated SOTP value is often described as a conglomerate discount. It may reflect complexity, capital-allocation concerns, holding-company costs, governance risk, limited disclosure or tax and regulatory friction. The discount may persist.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is SOTP suitable for every company?
No. It is most useful when distinct businesses or assets can be valued separately with reasonable information. A focused single-business company may be easier to value using a direct method.

### Should I use the same multiple for every division?
Not necessarily. The multiple should reflect each segment’s growth, profitability, capital intensity, risk and peer group.

### Is SOTP more accurate than DCF?
Neither method is automatically more accurate. SOTP is a framework for combining segment valuations; DCF is one possible method used to value a segment or the whole business.

### What is the biggest limitation?
The result can be highly sensitive to assumptions and the quality of segment disclosures. A precise-looking total can still be uncertain.
