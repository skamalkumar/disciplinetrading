## What Is Event Risk in Options?

Event risk is the possibility that a scheduled or unexpected announcement causes a sharp move in the underlying asset or a rapid change in market expectations. Options can be especially sensitive to such events because implied volatility and the expected range of prices may change before and after the announcement.

Examples include company earnings, central-bank policy decisions, inflation data, government budgets, elections, major economic releases and geopolitical developments.

## Why Implied Volatility May Rise Before an Event

When traders expect a significant announcement, demand for options can increase. Option prices may reflect a larger expected move, which can raise implied volatility. This is not automatic: the event may already be priced in, and volatility can differ across strikes and expiries.

## What Is a Volatility Crush?

After an event passes, uncertainty associated with that event may decline. Implied volatility can fall, sometimes sharply. This is often called a volatility crush.

A long option can lose value from falling implied volatility even if the underlying moves in the anticipated direction. The size of the effect depends on the option's Greeks, the actual price move, the volatility change and the time remaining.

## Illustrative Example

Suppose a trader buys a hypothetical NIFTY Call before a major policy announcement. The index rises after the announcement, but the option's implied volatility falls substantially. The directional gain may be partly or fully offset by the decline in volatility and the passage of time.

The example illustrates why a correct directional view does not guarantee a profitable long-option trade.

## Event Risk for Option Sellers

Option sellers may receive higher premiums before an event because implied volatility is elevated. However, the premium is compensation for accepting uncertainty. A large gap or unexpected announcement can cause losses that exceed the premium received, particularly for uncovered positions.

Defined-risk structures can cap the theoretical expiry loss, but they do not remove execution risk, liquidity risk or the possibility of rapid mark-to-market changes.

## The Implied Move Is Not a Forecast

Options prices can be used to estimate a market-implied move for a particular expiry, based on assumptions and a chosen calculation method. This is not a guaranteed range and does not tell the trader which direction the market will move. Actual moves can be smaller or much larger than the implied estimate.

## A Practical Event Checklist

1. Identify the exact event and announcement time.
2. Check which option expiry spans the event.
3. Compare implied volatility across expiries and strikes.
4. Review the option's Vega, Theta and Delta exposure.
5. Estimate the market-implied move and understand its assumptions.
6. Consider gap risk and the possibility of a move beyond the expected range.
7. Review liquidity, bid-ask spreads and the cost of exiting.
8. Decide whether holding through the event fits the strategy's risk limit.

## Indian Market Context

For NIFTY and SENSEX options, traders may monitor RBI policy announcements, Union Budget events, inflation releases, major global central-bank decisions and important domestic or global data. Confirm the event calendar and exchange expiry details from reliable current sources. An event may affect index constituents differently, and index-level volatility can change even when individual stocks move in different directions.

## Common Mistakes

- Assuming high implied volatility must fall after every event.
- Buying options without considering the premium already pricing in the event.
- Selling options only because premiums look expensive.
- Ignoring gaps and the possibility of an extreme outcome.
- Comparing IV across expiries without considering different event exposure.
- Treating the implied move as a hard upper or lower boundary.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do options always become cheaper after an announcement?

No. Implied volatility may fall, but a sufficiently large underlying move or a change in broader market conditions can still increase an option's value.

### Is it better to buy or sell options before an event?

There is no universally better choice. The decision depends on the price of volatility, expected move, payoff, risk tolerance and execution conditions.

### Which Greek is especially relevant to an IV change?

Vega measures an option's sensitivity to changes in implied volatility. Delta, Gamma and Theta also matter.

### Can an event move exceed the implied move?

Yes. The implied move is an estimate derived from market prices, not a maximum possible move.

## Final Thoughts

Economic events can affect both the underlying price and the volatility embedded in options. Evaluate the event, expiry, Greeks, implied move and potential gap risk together rather than relying on a directional forecast alone.
