## What Does Rolling an Options Position Mean?

Rolling an options position usually means closing an existing option position and opening another one, often with a different expiry, strike or both. It is effectively two linked transactions: an exit from the old contract and an entry into a new contract.

Rolling does not erase a loss on the original position. It changes the exposure from that point forward and may realise a gain or loss on the closed leg.

## Why Do Traders Roll?

A trader may consider rolling to:

- Move a position away from an approaching expiry
- Change the strike after the underlying price moves
- Extend the time available for a thesis to play out
- Adjust Delta or other portfolio exposures
- Re-establish a hedge in a later expiry

The reason should be explicit. “The position is losing, so I will roll it” is not, by itself, a risk-management plan.

## A Simple Example

Suppose a trader sold a Call option that is now close to expiry. The trader buys back that contract to close it and sells a Call in a later expiry. The new contract may have a different premium, Delta, Gamma, Theta and risk profile.

If the new sale generates more premium than the cost of closing the old option, the combined transaction may show a net credit. That credit does **not** automatically mean the roll is profitable overall. The original trade's realised result and the new position's future risk must both be included.

## Common Types of Rolls

### Roll out

Close the current contract and open a contract with a later expiry, often at the same strike.

### Roll up or down

Move to a higher or lower strike. This changes the position's sensitivity and payoff profile.

### Roll out and up/down

Change both expiry and strike. This can alter the risk materially, so compare the new position with the old one rather than treating it as a simple extension.

## Evaluate the New Trade, Not Just the Cash Flow

Before rolling, compare:

- Realised profit or loss on the position being closed
- Net debit or credit for the roll
- New maximum loss or relevant stress loss
- Net Delta, Gamma, Theta and Vega after the roll
- Additional margin and liquidity needs
- Remaining time to expiry and event risk
- Whether the original market thesis is still valid

A net credit can coexist with greater tail risk or a larger potential loss. A debit can sometimes reduce risk. Cash flow alone is not enough to judge the decision.

## Rolling vs Averaging Down

Rolling closes one contract and opens another. Averaging down usually means adding to an existing losing exposure or increasing exposure at a lower price. The terms are not interchangeable, although some rolls can increase overall exposure and become similar in effect to adding risk.

## Common Mistakes

- Rolling repeatedly to avoid recognising a loss
- Ignoring the risk of the new expiry
- Assuming a credit roll is automatically profitable
- Failing to recalculate margin and position Greeks
- Keeping a trade after its original rationale has failed
- Ignoring bid-ask spreads and execution costs

## A Disciplined Rolling Checklist

1. Define why the existing position should be closed.
2. Record its realised result separately.
3. Calculate the new position's payoff and risk before entry.
4. Compare the roll with closing the position and doing nothing.
5. Set a new exit, adjustment and maximum-loss plan.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does rolling guarantee more time to recover a loss?

It may move exposure to a later expiry, but it cannot guarantee recovery. The new position can lose money as well.

### Is a roll for a credit always better than a debit?

No. Credit or debit is only one part of the transaction. The new risk, payoff, costs and probability of outcomes matter.

### Should I roll every losing options trade?

No. Closing, reducing, hedging or rolling are different choices. The appropriate action depends on the original plan and the risk of the new position.
