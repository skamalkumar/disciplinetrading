## What Is an Order Book?

A company order book is the value or volume of customer orders that have been received but not yet executed, delivered or recognised as revenue, depending on the company’s reporting definition. It is often discussed in industries such as engineering, construction, infrastructure, defence, capital goods and project-based manufacturing.

An order book can provide visibility into potential future business. It is not the same as revenue, cash received or guaranteed profit.

## Order Inflows, Order Book and Execution

**Order inflows** are new orders received during a period. **Order book** is the outstanding value of orders at a point in time. **Execution** refers to the work performed or goods delivered against those orders. Revenue is recognised according to the applicable accounting standards and contract terms, not simply when an order is announced.

A simplified reconciliation is: Closing order book = opening order book + new orders − executed/cancelled/adjusted orders. Companies may define and report these components differently.

## Illustrative Example

Assume a company starts the year with an order book of ₹8,000 crore, receives new orders of ₹3,000 crore and executes ₹2,200 crore. If there are no cancellations or other adjustments, the closing order book would be ₹8,800 crore. This indicates a larger backlog, but does not by itself reveal the margin, timing or cash flow associated with the work.

## What Should Investors Examine?

1. **Order book-to-revenue:** Compare backlog with annual revenue, while recognising that execution schedules differ.
2. **Order inflow trend:** Check whether new orders replenish executed work.
3. **Customer and project concentration:** A few large orders can create dependence on individual customers or projects.
4. **Execution capability:** Assess capacity, labour, suppliers, project management and historical delivery.
5. **Margins:** Large orders can be low-margin or fixed-price contracts exposed to cost overruns.
6. **Payment terms:** Examine advances, milestone payments, retention money and receivable collection.
7. **Cancellations and delays:** Review the reasons for cancellations, extensions and disputes.
8. **Working capital:** Growth in execution can require inventory, contract assets and receivables before cash is collected.

## Why a Large Order Book Can Mislead

An order may be conditional, subject to approvals, financing or land acquisition. The headline value may include taxes, consortium shares or multi-year work. Some orders may be renegotiated, delayed or cancelled. Investors should read the company’s definition and exchange disclosures rather than compare headline numbers without context.

## Order Book and Valuation

A growing backlog may support revenue visibility, but valuation should ultimately reflect expected cash flows, margins, capital requirements, execution risk and return on capital. A company can grow its order book while destroying value if it accepts uneconomic contracts or cannot collect payments.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a higher order book always positive?
No. Quality, profitability, execution timing, customer credit and working-capital requirements matter.

### What is order book-to-sales ratio?
It compares outstanding orders with a period’s revenue. It is a rough visibility indicator, not a guaranteed number of years of revenue.

### Does an order win immediately become revenue?
Usually not. Revenue recognition depends on delivery or performance obligations under the applicable accounting framework.

### Which sectors use order book analysis?
It is commonly relevant to project-based and contract-driven businesses, including infrastructure, engineering, construction and capital goods.
