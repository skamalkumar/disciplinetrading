## What Is Quick Ratio?

The **Quick Ratio**, also called the **Acid-Test Ratio**, measures a company's ability to meet short-term liabilities using assets that can be converted into cash relatively quickly.

Unlike the Current Ratio, it generally excludes inventory because inventory may take time to sell.

The key question is:

> If the company had to meet its short-term obligations without relying heavily on inventory sales, would it have enough liquid assets?

## Quick Ratio Formula

**Quick Ratio = Quick Assets ÷ Current Liabilities**

A commonly used alternative is:

**Quick Ratio = (Current Assets − Inventory − Prepaid Expenses) ÷ Current Liabilities**

Quick assets generally include cash, cash equivalents, short-term investments and trade receivables.

## Example

Suppose a company has:

- Cash = ₹20 crore
- Receivables = ₹30 crore
- Short-term investments = ₹10 crore
- Inventory = ₹40 crore
- Current liabilities = ₹50 crore

Quick assets = ₹20 + ₹30 + ₹10 = ₹60 crore.

**Quick Ratio = ₹60 crore ÷ ₹50 crore = 1.20**

The company has ₹1.20 of quick assets for every ₹1 of current liabilities.

## Quick Ratio vs Current Ratio

The Current Ratio includes inventory:

**Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities**

Suppose current assets are ₹100 crore and current liabilities are ₹50 crore.

Current Ratio = **2.00**

If inventory is ₹40 crore, quick assets are ₹60 crore.

Quick Ratio = **1.20**

Therefore, the Quick Ratio gives a more conservative view of liquidity.

## What Does a Quick Ratio Above 1 Mean?

Broadly:

- Above 1: quick assets exceed current liabilities
- Around 1: quick assets roughly match current liabilities
- Below 1: the company may rely more on inventory conversion, operating cash flow or financing

These are not universal rules. Industry characteristics matter.

A retailer may naturally operate with a lower Quick Ratio because inventory is central to its business.

## Why Investors Should Compare Companies

The ratio is most useful when compared with:

- The company's own historical ratios
- Similar companies
- Industry norms

A company with a Quick Ratio of 1.5 is not automatically better than one at 0.9. The reason behind the difference matters.

## Quick Ratio and Receivables

Receivables are normally included in quick assets, but receivables are not the same as cash.

If customers are paying slowly, a high receivables balance can make the Quick Ratio look healthier than the actual cash position.

## Quick Ratio and Cash Flow

A company can have a reasonable Quick Ratio and still experience cash-flow pressure.

Investors should examine:

- Operating cash flow
- Free cash flow
- Receivables
- Short-term debt
- Working capital

## Advantages

- Conservative liquidity measure
- Excludes inventory
- Useful for peer comparison
- Helps identify short-term liquidity pressure

## Limitations

- Industry norms vary
- Receivables may not be collected quickly
- Does not measure profitability
- Does not measure long-term solvency
- A very high ratio can sometimes indicate excess idle cash

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a Quick Ratio above 1 always good?

No. It generally indicates stronger immediate liquidity, but the appropriate level depends on the business.

### Why is inventory excluded?

Inventory may require time to sell and may not convert into cash immediately.

### Can a company have a high Quick Ratio and still face problems?

Yes. Poor receivables collection or weak operating cash flow can create problems despite a healthy ratio.

## Final Thoughts

Quick Ratio is a useful fundamental-analysis tool for understanding short-term liquidity.

For equity investors, use it with profitability, leverage, cash flow and working-capital analysis rather than treating it as a standalone stock-selection signal.
