## What Is EPS?

Earnings Per Share, commonly called EPS, is one of the most widely used measures in equity analysis. It tells investors how much profit is attributable to each outstanding share of a company, based on the relevant accounting period and share count.

EPS helps investors connect a company's overall earnings with the individual shares they own. Instead of looking only at total net profit, an investor can use EPS to understand how much of that profit is attributable to each share.

## EPS Formula

A simplified basic EPS formula is:

**EPS = Profit Available to Common Shareholders ÷ Weighted Average Number of Outstanding Shares**

For example, suppose a company earns ₹100 crore of profit available to common shareholders and has a weighted average of 10 crore shares outstanding.

**EPS = ₹100 crore ÷ 10 crore = ₹10 per share**

The weighted average share count is important because the number of shares can change during the year through new share issues, buybacks, mergers, or other corporate actions.

## Why Is EPS Important?

EPS provides a useful way to compare a company's profitability on a per-share basis. A company may report higher total profits but still have weaker EPS growth if the number of shares has increased significantly.

Conversely, a company can sometimes increase EPS through share buybacks because the same level of earnings is spread across fewer shares.

This is why investors should examine both earnings growth and changes in the share count.

## Basic EPS vs Diluted EPS

Two common measures are basic EPS and diluted EPS.

### Basic EPS

Basic EPS generally uses the weighted average number of ordinary shares outstanding during the period.

### Diluted EPS

Diluted EPS considers the potential impact of securities that could increase the number of shares, such as certain stock options, convertible instruments, or other dilutive securities, subject to applicable accounting rules.

Diluted EPS can therefore provide a more conservative view of per-share earnings when potential dilution is material.

## EPS Growth Matters More Than One Number

A single EPS figure tells only part of the story. Investors should examine how EPS has changed over several years and compare the trend with revenue, operating profit, cash flow, and industry conditions.

For example, if a company's EPS rises from ₹8 to ₹10 and then ₹12 over three years, the direction of earnings is positive. But an investor should still investigate why EPS increased. Growth could come from stronger business performance, cost reduction, lower interest expenses, buybacks, or a combination of factors.

The quality and sustainability of EPS growth matter.

## EPS and the P/E Ratio

EPS is directly connected to the Price-to-Earnings ratio, or P/E ratio.

**P/E Ratio = Market Price per Share ÷ EPS**

Suppose a stock trades at ₹300 and its EPS is ₹20.

**P/E = ₹300 ÷ ₹20 = 15**

This means investors are paying ₹15 for every ₹1 of the company's annual earnings per share, based on the EPS measure being used.

P/E should not be used alone. A company with a higher P/E may have stronger expected growth, better business quality, or lower perceived risk. A low P/E may indicate attractive valuation, but it can also reflect weak growth expectations or business risks.

## EPS Growth vs Revenue Growth

Revenue growth and EPS growth should be analysed together. If revenue rises rapidly but EPS remains flat, increasing costs, interest expenses, taxes, dilution, or other factors may be absorbing the additional revenue.

A healthy fundamental analysis therefore asks:

- Is revenue growing?
- Is operating profit growing?
- Is net profit growing?
- Is EPS growing?
- Is free cash flow supporting reported earnings?
- Is the share count increasing or decreasing?

## Limitations of EPS

EPS is useful, but it has limitations. It is an accounting measure and can be affected by accounting policies, exceptional items, acquisitions, buybacks, and changes in the share count.

EPS also does not tell you whether a stock is cheap or expensive by itself. A company with ₹20 EPS is not automatically cheaper than a company with ₹10 EPS because the market prices of the two companies may be very different.

## How Beginners Can Use EPS

A disciplined investor can use EPS as one part of a broader fundamental analysis framework. Look at multi-year EPS growth, compare the company with its peers, examine the P/E ratio, and investigate whether earnings growth is supported by the underlying business and cash generation.

For Indian equities, EPS can be particularly useful when analysing listed companies alongside revenue growth, operating margins, return on equity, debt levels, and valuation.

## Final Thoughts

EPS is a simple but powerful starting point for understanding company profitability on a per-share basis. However, the best stock analysis does not stop at a single EPS number. Investors should study the trend, understand the drivers of growth, consider dilution, and compare EPS with cash flow and valuation measures.

Used correctly, EPS can help investors move from simply looking at a share price to understanding the economics of the business behind the share.
