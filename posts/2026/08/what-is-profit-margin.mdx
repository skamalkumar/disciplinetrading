## What Is Profit Margin?

Profit margin is a financial ratio showing how much profit a company generates from its revenue.

The basic formula is:

**Profit Margin = Profit ÷ Revenue × 100**

Revenue tells us how much business a company generates. Profit margin tells us how much of that revenue remains as profit after relevant costs.

For the revenue side of this analysis, read [What Is Revenue? A Beginner's Guide to Company Sales and Growth](https://www.disciplinetrading.com/blog/what-is-revenue).

## Why Is Profit Margin Important?

Two companies can generate the same revenue but produce very different profits.

Suppose Company A has revenue of ₹1,000 crore and profit of ₹200 crore.

Its margin is:

**₹200 crore ÷ ₹1,000 crore × 100 = 20%**

Company B also has revenue of ₹1,000 crore but profit of ₹50 crore.

Its margin is only 5%.

The difference tells investors something about the efficiency and economics of the two businesses.

## The Three Main Profit Margins

Investors commonly examine:

1. Gross profit margin
2. Operating profit margin
3. Net profit margin

Each measures profitability at a different stage of the income statement.

## Gross Profit Margin

Gross profit is revenue minus the direct costs associated with producing goods or delivering services.

The simplified formula is:

**Gross Profit Margin = Gross Profit ÷ Revenue × 100**

Suppose revenue is ₹1,000 crore and gross profit is ₹400 crore.

Gross margin is 40%.

Gross margin can provide information about pricing power, direct input costs and the economics of the company's products or services.

## Operating Profit Margin

Operating profit margin measures profitability after operating expenses but before items such as interest and taxes.

The simplified formula is:

**Operating Profit Margin = Operating Profit ÷ Revenue × 100**

If revenue is ₹1,000 crore and operating profit is ₹150 crore:

**₹150 crore ÷ ₹1,000 crore × 100 = 15%**

Operating margin helps investors understand the profitability of the core operating business.

## Net Profit Margin

Net profit margin measures the portion of revenue that remains as net profit after relevant expenses.

The simplified formula is:

**Net Profit Margin = Net Profit ÷ Revenue × 100**

If revenue is ₹1,000 crore and net profit is ₹100 crore:

**₹100 crore ÷ ₹1,000 crore × 100 = 10%**

## A Simple Example

Imagine a company reports:

| Measure | Amount | Margin |
|---|---:|---:|
| Revenue | ₹1,000 crore | — |
| Gross Profit | ₹400 crore | 40% |
| Operating Profit | ₹150 crore | 15% |
| Net Profit | ₹100 crore | 10% |

This progression shows how costs reduce profitability as we move down the income statement.

## Margin Expansion

Margin expansion occurs when profit increases faster than revenue.

Suppose revenue rises from ₹1,000 crore to ₹1,200 crore while operating profit rises from ₹100 crore to ₹150 crore.

Operating margin improves from 10% to 12.5%.

Possible reasons include:

- Higher selling prices
- Lower input costs
- Better capacity utilisation
- Productivity improvements
- Economies of scale
- Better product mix

## Margin Compression

Margin compression is the opposite.

Suppose revenue rises from ₹1,000 crore to ₹1,200 crore but operating profit rises only from ₹100 crore to ₹105 crore.

Operating margin falls from 10% to 8.75%.

The company is growing sales but becoming less profitable relative to revenue.

This is why investors should analyse revenue and margins together.

## Profit Margin and EPS

Profit margins influence earnings.

If revenue increases and margins remain stable, profits may grow broadly with revenue.

If revenue increases and margins expand, profit can grow faster than revenue.

If revenue increases but margins contract, earnings growth can disappoint.

This connects profitability with EPS.

## Profit Margin and ROE

Profit margin is also connected to return on equity, although ROE includes other factors such as asset efficiency and financial leverage.

A company can have strong margins but weak ROE if it uses capital inefficiently.

Another company may have moderate margins but attractive ROE because it uses its assets and equity efficiently.

## Why Industry Comparison Matters

Profit margins vary significantly between industries.

A retailer, manufacturer, technology company and financial company can have very different normal margin structures.

Therefore, investors should compare:

- The company's current margin with its own history.
- The company's margin with relevant competitors.
- The company's margin with the broader industry where appropriate.

Comparing unrelated businesses only by margin can lead to misleading conclusions.

## Profit Margin and Competitive Advantage

Sustainable margins can sometimes indicate competitive advantages.

A company with strong brands, pricing power, efficient operations, network effects or cost advantages may maintain stronger margins than competitors.

However, unusually high margins can also be temporary.

Investors should investigate whether high profitability comes from:

- One-off gains
- Temporary pricing conditions
- Unusually low input costs
- Asset sales
- Exceptional market conditions

The goal is to identify sustainable profitability rather than temporary numbers.

## Profit Margin and P/E Ratio

Profitability and valuation are related but different.

P/E compares market price with earnings.

A company with strong margins may generate attractive earnings growth, but investors still need to consider how much they are paying for those earnings.

Read [What Is P/E Ratio? A Beginner's Guide](https://www.disciplinetrading.com/blog/what-is-pe-ratio).

A high-quality company can still be an expensive investment if its valuation is excessive.

## Profit Margin for NIFTY 50 and SENSEX Investors

Investors analysing companies represented in NIFTY 50 and SENSEX can use profit margins to compare business quality within relevant sectors.

The most useful comparison is usually the company's margin trend over time and its position relative to comparable businesses.

Investors should also examine whether margin improvement is supported by revenue growth and cash generation.

## Common Mistakes Beginners Make

### Looking Only at Net Margin

Gross and operating margins can reveal where profitability is changing.

### Ignoring Margin Trends

One year's margin may not represent the long-term economics of the business.

### Comparing Unrelated Industries

Different business models naturally produce different margins.

### Assuming High Margin Means Low Risk

Margins can decline when competition or input costs change.

### Ignoring Cash Flow

Accounting profit does not automatically equal cash generation.

## Final Thoughts

Profit margin is one of the simplest ways to understand how efficiently a company converts revenue into profit.

A useful framework is:

**Revenue → Gross Profit → Operating Profit → Net Profit → EPS**

Then combine profitability with ROE, debt, cash flow and valuation.

That provides a much more complete picture than looking at the share price alone.
