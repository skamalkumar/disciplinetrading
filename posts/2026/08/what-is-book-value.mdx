## What Is Book Value?

Book value represents the accounting value of a company's net assets after subtracting its liabilities from its assets.

In simplified form:

**Book Value = Total Assets − Total Liabilities**

It provides an accounting-based view of the net value attributable to shareholders.

Book value is different from the market value of a company.

To understand market value, see [What Is Market Capitalization?](https://disciplinetrading.com/blog/what-is-market-capitalization).

## What Is Book Value Per Share?

Book value per share, often abbreviated as **BVPS**, measures the company's book value on a per-share basis.

A simplified formula is:

**Book Value Per Share = Shareholders' Equity ÷ Number of Outstanding Shares**

For example, if a company has shareholders' equity of ₹5,000 crore and 100 crore outstanding shares:

**BVPS = ₹50 per share**

This does not mean the stock must trade at ₹50.

The market price can be substantially above or below book value per share.

## Book Value vs Share Price

Share price is determined by the market and reflects investors' expectations about the company's future.

Book value is based primarily on accounting values reported on the balance sheet.

Therefore:

- Market price reflects what investors are willing to pay.
- Book value reflects accounting net assets.
- The difference can be significant.

A company trading at ₹150 with a BVPS of ₹50 has a price-to-book ratio of 3.

## What Is Price-to-Book Ratio?

The Price-to-Book ratio, or **P/B ratio**, compares a company's market price with its book value per share.

A simplified formula is:

**P/B Ratio = Market Price Per Share ÷ Book Value Per Share**

If a stock trades at ₹150 and its BVPS is ₹50:

**P/B = 3**

This means the market values the company's equity at three times its accounting book value per share.

## Why Investors Look at Book Value

Book value can be useful when analysing companies where balance-sheet assets are important.

It is particularly relevant in areas such as financial institutions and asset-heavy businesses, although its usefulness varies by industry.

It should not be treated as a universal measure of intrinsic value.

## Book Value and ROE

Book value and Return on Equity are closely related concepts.

ROE measures how effectively a company generates profit from shareholders' equity.

See [What Is ROE?](https://disciplinetrading.com/blog/what-is-roe) for a detailed explanation.

A company with a high ROE may be generating strong returns on its equity base.

However, investors should examine the reason for a high ROE, including leverage and changes in the equity base.

## Book Value and EPS

Book value and EPS measure different aspects of a company.

[What Is EPS?](https://disciplinetrading.com/blog/what-is-eps) measures earnings attributable to each share.

Book value per share measures accounting net assets attributable to each share.

Both can contribute to a broader fundamental-analysis framework.

## Book Value Can Be Negative

A company can have negative book value when its total liabilities exceed its assets.

This can make traditional P/B analysis less meaningful.

Therefore, investors should not mechanically apply P/B ratios to every company.

## Book Value and Intangible Assets

Accounting book value can differ significantly from what investors perceive as the economic value of a company.

Technology, brand-driven and service businesses may have substantial intangible assets that are not represented in the same way as physical assets on the balance sheet.

As a result, a low P/B ratio does not automatically mean that a stock is undervalued.

## Book Value and Market Capitalization

Market capitalisation reflects the market value of outstanding shares.

Book value reflects accounting net assets.

Comparing the two can help investors understand how the market values the company's equity relative to its accounting value.

For the broader concept of company size and market value, see [What Is Market Capitalization?](https://disciplinetrading.com/blog/what-is-market-capitalization).

## How Beginners Can Use Book Value

A disciplined process is:

1. Find shareholders' equity on the balance sheet.
2. Check the number of outstanding shares.
3. Calculate or verify book value per share.
4. Compare the market price with BVPS.
5. Calculate or review the P/B ratio.
6. Compare the ratio with relevant industry peers.
7. Examine ROE, earnings and debt.
8. Understand the company's business model before drawing a conclusion.

## Book Value Is Not a Guaranteed Floor

One of the most common mistakes is assuming that book value represents a guaranteed minimum stock price.

It does not.

Assets may not be worth their reported accounting values in a stressed situation, and liabilities, asset quality and future profitability all matter.

Market prices can therefore remain below book value for extended periods.

## Final Thoughts

Book value provides investors with an accounting-based measure of shareholders' net assets.

Book value per share and the P/B ratio can be useful tools, especially when combined with ROE, earnings, debt and industry comparisons.

But like any single financial metric, book value should be used as part of a broader fundamental-analysis process.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is book value?

Book value is the accounting value of a company's assets minus its liabilities.

### What is book value per share?

Book value per share is shareholders' equity divided by the number of outstanding shares.

### Is a stock below book value automatically cheap?

No. A stock can trade below book value for valid business, profitability, asset-quality or industry reasons.

### Is book value useful for every company?

Its usefulness varies by industry. It is generally more informative for some asset-heavy and financial businesses than for businesses whose value is driven heavily by intangible assets.
