## What Is Option Expiry?

Option expiry is the date on which an options contract reaches the end of its contractual life. After expiry, that particular contract is no longer available for trading.

For an options trader, expiry is important because the amount of time remaining directly affects option value. As expiry approaches, the time component of an option's premium generally declines, all else being equal.

To understand the basic structure first, read [What Are Options? A Beginner's Guide to Call and Put Options](https://www.disciplinetrading.com/blog/what-are-options).

## Why Does Expiry Matter?

An option has a limited lifespan. Unlike a share, an option does not continue indefinitely.

This means an options trader must consider not only direction, strike price, premium and volatility, but also:

**How much time is left?**

An option buyer may be correct about market direction but still lose money if the expected move does not happen quickly enough.

## Expiry and Time Value

Option premium can be broadly understood as containing intrinsic value and time value.

**Option Premium = Intrinsic Value + Time Value**

For an introduction, read [What Is Intrinsic Value and Time Value?](https://www.disciplinetrading.com/blog/what-is-intrinsic-and-time-value).

Time value represents the additional value associated with the possibility that the option may become more valuable before expiry. As expiry approaches, there is less time for that possibility to play out, so time value generally declines.

## What Happens to an Option at Expiry?

At expiry, the option's final value depends on the relationship between the underlying price, strike price and option type.

For a Call, intrinsic value at expiry is based on how far the underlying is above the strike.

For a Put, intrinsic value at expiry is based on how far the underlying is below the strike.

The exact settlement mechanics depend on the contract specifications of the exchange and instrument. Always verify the current exchange rules before trading.

## ITM, ATM and OTM at Expiry

The relationship between the underlying and strike determines whether an option finishes in-the-money, at-the-money or out-of-the-money.

For a Call:

- **ITM:** Underlying price is above the strike.
- **ATM:** Underlying price is approximately at the strike.
- **OTM:** Underlying price is below the strike.

For a Put:

- **ITM:** Underlying price is below the strike.
- **ATM:** Underlying price is approximately at the strike.
- **OTM:** Underlying price is above the strike.

For more detail, read [What Are ITM, ATM and OTM Options?](https://www.disciplinetrading.com/blog/what-are-itm-atm-otm-options).

## What Happens to an OTM Option at Expiry?

If an option finishes out-of-the-money at expiry, it has no intrinsic value.

For a buyer who purchased that option, the premium paid can therefore be lost.

Imagine a hypothetical NIFTY Call with a strike of 25,500. If NIFTY finishes below 25,500 at expiry, that Call is out-of-the-money and has no intrinsic value at expiry.

This is why option buyers need to consider both direction and timing.

## What Happens to an ITM Option at Expiry?

An in-the-money option has intrinsic value at expiry.

Suppose a hypothetical NIFTY Call has:

- Strike = 25,000
- NIFTY expiry level = 25,200

The intrinsic value is approximately:

**25,200 − 25,000 = 200 points**

The actual settlement amount depends on the applicable contract specifications.

For the strike-price concept, read [What Is Strike Price? A Beginner's Guide](https://www.disciplinetrading.com/blog/what-is-strike-price).

## Expiry and Option Buyers

Option buyers pay a premium for the rights associated with the contract. Their maximum loss on a purchased option is generally limited to the premium paid, excluding transaction costs.

However, the underlying needs to move sufficiently and within the available time for the option to become profitable after considering the premium and costs.

For the other side of the trade, read [Option Buyer vs Option Seller](https://www.disciplinetrading.com/blog/option-buyer-vs-seller).

## Expiry and Option Sellers

Option sellers receive premium but take on an obligation under the contract.

As expiry approaches, time decay can work in favour of an option seller when other factors are unchanged. However, selling options can involve substantial risk when the underlying makes a large or unexpected move.

Expiry should therefore never be treated as a reason to sell options without understanding risk.

## Expiry and Theta

Theta measures the sensitivity of an option's value to the passage of time.

As expiry approaches, the impact of time decay can become increasingly important, especially for options close to the money.

For the factors affecting option prices, read [What Is an Option Premium?](https://www.disciplinetrading.com/blog/what-is-an-option-premium).

Expiry therefore connects directly with one of the most important concepts in options trading:

**Time is part of the trade.**

## Expiry and Implied Volatility

Expiry also interacts with implied volatility.

Option premium is influenced by expected volatility as well as time remaining, strike and the underlying price. An increase in implied volatility can increase option premiums, while a fall in implied volatility can reduce them, all else being equal.

Read [What Is Implied Volatility? A Beginner's Guide to IV in Options Trading](https://www.disciplinetrading.com/blog/what-is-implied-volatility).

## NIFTY Option Expiry

NIFTY 50 has a defined derivatives contract structure on NSE, including weekly and monthly option contracts. NSE's current contract information should be checked for the exact available expiries and applicable rules before trading.

The practical lesson is simple:

**Always confirm the exact expiry date of the contract you are trading.**

Do not assume that an expiry schedule remains unchanged forever.

## SENSEX and Expiry

SENSEX derivatives are also subject to exchange-defined contract specifications.

As with NIFTY, traders should verify the current contract details, expiry schedule, settlement method and other specifications before placing a trade.

This is particularly important when positions are held close to expiry.

## Expiry-Day Behaviour

Expiry sessions can behave differently from ordinary sessions because traders and institutions are managing positions that have a fixed contractual end date.

Possible features include:

- Position adjustments
- Hedging activity
- Option premium changes
- Rapid changes in OI
- Sensitivity around major strikes
- Fast intraday movements

These are observations, not guarantees. Expiry does not automatically mean that the market will become more volatile.

## A Simple Expiry Checklist

Before carrying an options position toward expiry, ask:

1. What is the exact expiry date?
2. What is the underlying price?
3. What is my strike?
4. Is the option ITM, ATM or OTM?
5. How much premium did I pay or receive?
6. How much time value remains?
7. What is the current implied volatility?
8. What is the Open Interest structure?
9. What is my maximum planned loss?
10. What is my exit plan?

This checklist can help prevent decisions based purely on hope.

## Common Mistakes Beginners Make

### Forgetting the Expiry Date

Every option has a limited life.

### Buying Cheap OTM Options Without Considering Time

A low premium does not automatically mean a good opportunity.

### Ignoring Theta

Time decay can become significant as expiry approaches.

### Holding Because the Market Is "Eventually" Going to Move

Options have a deadline. Being right about direction at the wrong time can still result in a loss.

### Ignoring Contract Specifications

Always verify current exchange rules and settlement details.

## Final Thoughts

Expiry is one of the fundamental differences between options and ordinary shares.

An option is a time-limited contract, which means the trader must manage both direction and time.

Understanding expiry makes concepts such as strike price, intrinsic value, time value, theta, implied volatility and option premium much easier to understand.

The disciplined approach is not to fear expiry, but to understand exactly what happens to the contract as expiry approaches.
